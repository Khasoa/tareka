A bag of rubbish sits beside a drain in a Nairobi neighbourhood. The collection truck didn't come this week, and it may not come next week either. For now, nothing seems to happen.

Then it rains.

The thing is, waste rarely stays where we leave it. What looks like a small disposal problem can turn into a drainage problem, a health problem and a money problem, often in places far from where the waste started. That chain is why poor waste management is a public health issue as much as an environmental one.

## It starts with a gap in collection

Waste doesn't need to be dumped deliberately to cause harm. It only needs to go uncollected.

A study by the [African Population and Health Research Centre (APHRC)](https://www.iied.org/21726g), examining households in Nairobi and Mombasa, found that when regular collection was unavailable or unreliable, residents sometimes resorted to dumping waste in rivers, drainage trenches, along railway lines and roadsides, or burning it.

In Nairobi's Korogocho and Dandora neighbourhoods, the study recorded household waste disposal in rivers, railway areas and drainage trenches, alongside open burning. These practices show what can happen when the formal waste-management system does not reach everyone.

When there is no reliable system, people improvise. The consequences do not necessarily stay within the household or neighbourhood where the waste was generated.

## Plastic moves with the water

Plastic bags, bottles and food packaging are light, durable and easily carried by wind and flowing water. Once discarded in streets, open spaces or waterways, they can travel far from where they were thrown away.

Research on plastic pollution in the [Nairobi River Basin](https://kesebae.or.ke/journal/index.php/kesebae/article/view/119) has documented plastic waste entering the river system. Waste accumulating in drainage channels adds to the obstruction of stormwater flows.

It would be too simple to blame plastic alone for flooding. Nairobi's drainage problems also involve infrastructure, maintenance, silt accumulation and development on riparian land. But waste in drainage channels makes an existing problem worse. When stormwater cannot drain properly, homes, businesses and roads are affected.

## Organic waste does its own damage

Food scraps and garden waste behave differently from plastic. Dumped in bulk, they decompose, attract pests and can produce contaminated liquid that seeps through surrounding waste and into the environment.

They also generate methane as they break down in landfills and open dumps. Methane is a powerful greenhouse gas, making poorly managed organic waste a climate concern as well as a local environmental problem. The [United Nations Environment Programme](https://www.unep.org/en/what-we-do/food-systems/nature-positive-resilient-agriculture/ghg-slcp-mitigation-particular) identifies better management of organic waste as part of efforts to reduce methane emissions.

There is another consequence that receives less attention: organic waste can contaminate dry materials around it. Paper, cardboard and some plastics become harder to recover when mixed with decomposing food and other wet waste.

Organic waste is not simply a nuisance to be hidden. Handled well, it can become compost or be used in other recovery processes. Handled badly, it contributes to pollution and reduces the value of materials around it.

## Electronics carry the hidden hazard

Phones, batteries, cables and old appliances contain materials that can be harmful when handled or processed unsafely. Some electronic components contain lead, mercury and other hazardous substances.

Electronics also have recoverable value. That is why they are sometimes dismantled to retrieve metals and other components. But open burning, unsafe dismantling and poorly controlled disposal can expose workers and surrounding communities to harmful substances.

The danger isn't always visible, and it falls particularly heavily on people doing the dismantling and those living nearby. That is why electronics need a different route from ordinary household rubbish.

Kenya's National Environment Management Authority identifies unsafe dismantling, open burning and improper disposal as concerns in its [E-waste Management Guidelines](https://nema.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/E-WASTE-GUIDELINES.pdf).

## Glass and metal injure, but in different ways

Broken glass and sharp scrap metal can cut the people who handle waste, especially those working without gloves or protective footwear.

Metal often has resale value, so it may be recovered early. Glass can be heavier and less attractive to some buyers, meaning it may be left behind. Both materials can be recovered, but the available buyers and processing routes vary.

Keeping them separate reduces the risk of injury during handling and makes it easier to identify suitable recovery routes.

## Paper and cardboard are easily spoiled

Paper and cardboard are less likely to present the same handling hazards as e-waste, but they still create problems when poorly managed.

Dry, they burn easily. Wet and mixed with food scraps, they lose their structure and much of their value for recycling. Cardboard that could have been collected and sold can instead become part of the waste accumulating in drains or dumpsites.

For these materials, keeping them dry and separate makes a practical difference.

## Clothes and textiles are easy to overlook

Textile waste doesn't always look dangerous, which is partly why it gets ignored. Worn-out clothes, fabric offcuts and unsellable textiles can accumulate when there are few practical routes for reuse or recovery.

Some fabrics contain synthetic fibres made from plastics. When discarded textiles are mixed with wet waste, they become harder to reuse, repair or recover. When burned, synthetic materials contribute to air pollution.

Clothes that could have been resold, mended or repurposed can end up as one more heap that someone has to deal with.

## Fire is the cheapest disposal, and one of the costliest

Where waste isn't collected, burning can become an easy way to make it disappear. But mixed waste burned in the open produces smoke containing pollutants that people nearby breathe in.

The [World Health Organization identifies open waste burning](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09367) as a source of air pollution with harmful effects on human health and the climate. The problem is not limited to large dumpsites. Burning can take place in streets, household compounds and smaller dumping areas.

Dandora, Nairobi's main dumpsite, illustrates the risks faced by people who work around waste over long periods. A [biomedical study of waste workers at Dandora](https://www.iied.org/21646g), conducted by researchers from APHRC and partner institutions, examined infections, injuries and other health concerns associated with direct exposure to waste. The researchers documented frequent injuries and infections among workers, alongside limited access to complete protective equipment.

Separately, a [2007 assessment commissioned by the United Nations Environment Programme](https://wedocs.unep.org/handle/20.500.11822/8744) examined environmental contamination around Dandora, including heavy metals in soil and water samples, and investigated potential health risks to nearby communities.

These findings do not mean that every person living or working near waste will experience the same health effects. They do show why unmanaged waste and open burning cannot be treated as merely an unpleasant sight.

## Someone always pays

The cost of unmanaged waste rarely lands on the person who created it. Households clean up after floods. Counties spend money clearing drains. Waste pickers who work at Dandora take on exposure that most of us never see.

There is a loss of opportunity as well. Bottles, cans, cardboard, textiles and scrap that could have been collected clean and sold on instead end up mixed, wet or contaminated. The recovery economy loses material, and people lose income.

## Where recovery fits in

Recovery doesn't solve every part of this chain, but it creates another route for materials that would otherwise be discarded. Material that is separated and delivered to a suitable recovery business has a better chance of being reused, recycled or processed rather than mixed with general waste.

That is why Tareka's directory is organised around materials. Different materials need different destinations, so we group them into plastics, glass, metals, organic waste, paper and cardboard, e-waste and textiles. Each has its own collectors, recyclers and makers, and each can go wrong in its own way when it is left unmanaged.

Separation helps, but it can't replace missing collection services, safe disposal sites or enforcement. Households, businesses, collectors, counties and recovery operators all have a part to play.

## What happens after you let go

Throwing something away feels like the end of a decision. It's actually the start of a journey, and the route depends on a system most of us rarely see. When the system works, a bottle can become material again. When it doesn't, waste can end up in a drain, in smoke or in a stranger's lungs.

The next time you let go of something, it's worth asking where it will go.
