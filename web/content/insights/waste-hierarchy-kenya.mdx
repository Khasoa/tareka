Waste moves through a city long after someone throws it away. It passes through bins (and, too often, gutters), collectors’ carts, trucks, sorting areas, and buyers’ hands. At each stage, someone decides what it is worth and where it goes next. Many of the people who recover value from discarded materials never meet the person who threw them away.

The waste hierarchy ranks the options for dealing with waste, from most to least preferred: prevent it, keep things in use, recover what you can, and dispose of the rest safely.

**Where the idea comes from**

The European Community’s 1975 Waste Framework Directive established an early framework for waste management. The familiar ranked hierarchy emerged in 1979, when Dutch MP Ad Lansink proposed a ladder placing prevention above disposal.

In 2008, the EU formally established a five-step hierarchy: prevention, preparing for reuse, recycling, other recovery and disposal.

Kenya’s [Sustainable Waste Management Act, 2022](https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/2022/31/eng@2022-12-31#part_IV) uses a more detailed sequence of eight priorities: avoidance, reduction, reuse, repair, refurbishment, recycling, recovery, and finally treatment for safe disposal.

### A ladder built for a different starting point

The hierarchy assumes a system underneath it: collection, sorting and someone to buy what is recovered. In much of Kenya, that system remains incomplete.

Dandora, one of Kenya’s largest dumpsites, continues to receive an estimated [2,000 tonnes](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/mar/08/waste-pickers-kenya-recycling-dandora-rubbish-dump) of waste a day, despite having been declared full in [2001](https://africanarguments.org/2026/07/what-happens-after-dandora-kenyas-green-transition-faces-its-hardest-test/).

The Kenya National Bureau of Statistics’ [Economic Survey 2026](https://www.knbs.or.ke/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/2026-Economic-Survey.pdf) reports that in 2025, Nairobi collected 33.2 per cent of its reported solid waste, compared with 51.9 per cent in Mombasa and 30 per cent in Kisumu. Waste that is not collected can end up dumped illegally, burned or washed into drains.

Across Africa, UNEP’s 2018 [Africa Waste Management Outlook](https://www.ccacoalition.org/resources/africa-waste-management-outlook-full-report) estimated that waste collection averaged about 55 per cent, with more than 90 per cent of waste dumped or openly burned.

That changes what the bottom of the ladder looks like. The Act ends with “treatment for safe disposal”. An overfull, unlined dumpsite or a roadside heap isn’t that. It sits below the last rung.

The Act provides for regulations on converting dumpsites into landfills. It also requires counties to provide collection centres for recyclables and materials recovery facilities, and to encourage separation at source in neighbourhoods and informal settlements.

### What already happens without the law

Waste pickers have practised parts of the hierarchy for decades, often without official recognition. At Dandora, they recover plastics, metals, paper, textiles, glass, electronics and other materials, selling them to aggregators, companies and individual buyers.

A [May 2025 study by the Kenya–Denmark Strategic Sector Cooperation, conducted with the Nairobi Recyclable Waste Association](https://eng.mst.dk/media/zlba3iep/ssc-report-wastepickers-at-the-dandora-dumpsite-may-2025.pdf), found that 49 per cent of the 100 waste pickers interviewed estimated their daily earnings at KSh 300–500. Another 30 per cent earned less than KSh 300. Earnings depended on what arrived, and some workers went home with nothing.

The work also carries serious health risks. The study documented respiratory problems, infections, cuts and burns among waste pickers, many of whom worked without protective equipment. Access to clean water, sanitation and healthcare was limited. Some workers also reported violence, bullying and sexual harassment.

In February 2026, the [Environment and Land Court](https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/judgment/keelc/2026/1158/eng@2026-02-05) awarded KSh 25,000 each to five waste pickers and the 1,032 pickers they represented, to be paid by Nairobi County Government.

The court found the dumpsite’s conditions hazardous and the harm ongoing, but held that the pickers had not proved their individual illnesses were caused by the pollution. The award was for the violation of their environmental rights, not for personal injury.

In [March 2026](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/2026-03-10-new-reforms-to-formally-recognise-waste-pickers), Environment Cabinet Secretary Deborah Barasa said the government intended to recognise and support waste workers following consultations with waste pickers in Nairobi.

### When the ladder moves, someone feels it

Climbing the hierarchy can affect people whose livelihoods depend on recovering waste. When materials are separated before they reach Dandora, fewer valuable items may arrive with the trucks, leaving waste pickers with less to collect and sell.

In the May 2025 study, 86 of the 100 waste pickers interviewed said their economic situation had worsened in recent years.

They pointed to declining recyclable material reaching the dumpsite, increasing competition, fewer truck arrivals and rising living costs. Some also reported health problems that limited how much they could collect, while inconsistent payments and lower prices for recovered materials added to the pressure.

This does not mean separation at source should be abandoned. It means that shifting where materials are sorted changes who gets access to them. Better recovery systems need to account for waste pickers’ livelihoods, safer working conditions and participation in formal waste management arrangements.

### What the law now asks of you

Section 20 of the Act requires anyone who generates waste to separate it at source and hand it only to licensed waste service providers or designated collection points. A person convicted of failing to comply faces a fine of up to KSh 20,000, imprisonment for up to six months, or both.

Under the [2024 Waste Management Regulations (Legal Notice 178 of 2024)](https://nema.go.ke/images/Docs/Regulations%202024%20Gazetted/WASTE%20MANAGEMENT%20AND%20SAND%20HARVESTING%20REGULATIONS-LN178-179_.pdf), waste is separated into colour-coded containers: green for organic waste, blue for recyclables and black for general waste.

In September 2025, NEMA [directed county governments to reorganise designated dumpsites](https://nema.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/WASTE-SEGREGATION-min.pdf), including by setting aside separate reception areas for organic waste, general waste, residual waste from materials recovery facilities and waste destined for landfill.

A household can separate perfectly and still find nobody collects the blue bag.

### Using the hierarchy

Next time you’re about to discard something, ask in order:

1. Did I need it, or its packaging, in the first place?
2. Can it still be used, by me or by someone else?
3. Can its material be recovered, and does anyone nearby take it?
4. If not, what is the proper disposal route?

The best step is usually the highest one you can actually reach.
